## Part H: Craft

### H1. Pins first or tails first

- **For pins first:** The finished pin board stands steady on the tail board lying flat. The transfer then lands on long grain and is easy to see. Small saw errors on the pins do not spoil the joint, because the tails are fitted to them [H 1]. On large carcases, standing the board up is more convenient [H 2]. Pins are considered easier to saw square [H 5].
- **For tails first:** Tail boards can be stacked and sawn together [H 1][H 2]. The spacing is easier to plan [H 5]. Several current guides work this way [H 11][H 13].
- **Traditions:** European-trained cabinetmakers are seen as the pins-first school [H 3]. Frid and Becksvoort represent the two positions [H 1]. North Bennet Street School and Frank Klausz cut pins first [H 4][H 12][H 16].
- **Bottom line:** You cannot see the order on the finished joint [H 1]. Rule of thumb: cut first the half that transfers more easily to the other [H 2]. The course follows the textbook: pins first.

### H2. Spacing

- **Half pins:** There is a half pin at each edge. Cosman places it about 6 mm (1/4″) from the edge [H 13].
- **Laying out:** With dividers at one setting for the whole board [H 11][H 13]. Experienced makers space by eye; some irregularity is part of the look [H 12].
- **Chisel width:** The spacing can be matched to the chisels on hand. Otherwise a second cut is needed, and the baseline easily turns wavy [H 22]. Common widths in the workshop are 12 and 24 mm [H 23].
- **Textbook (unverified, title pending):** Geometric spacing on the center line. Number of tails AZS estimated, checked with B/(1.7·D) to B/(2·D). Number of parts AZT = 3·AZS + 1, part T = B/AZT. Pins 1T, tails 2T, a half pin at each edge. The calculator in the course implements this.
- **Mark the waste:** Mark each waste piece with an X before sawing [H 5][H 11][H 15].

### H3. Slope 1:6 and 1:8

**Conversion** (arctan of the ratio, our own calculation):

| Ratio | Angle |
|---|---|
| 1:8 | ≈ 7.13° |
| 1:7 | ≈ 8.13° |
| 1:6 | ≈ 9.46° |
| 1:5 | ≈ 11.31° |
| 1:4 | ≈ 14.04° |

- **1:6 is not 10–14°.** A range of 10° to 14° corresponds to about 1:5.7 to 1:4. If the textbook gives 10–14° in addition to 1:6, that is a second rule of thumb, not the same value. The course uses 1:6 ≈ 9.46° (rounded to 9.5° in the text).
- **Common rule:** 1:6 for softwood, 1:8 for hardwood, 1:7 as a compromise [H 10]. Woodsmith recommends 1:8 to 1:6 [H 5].
- **Figures given in degrees, listed separately:**
  - Huey: 9° for softwood, 7° for hardwood; he himself uses 12° [H 8].
  - Hedges: antique furniture about 7° to 14° [H 9].
  - Forest Products Laboratory, per Hedges: tested 7.5° to 17.5°, no difference in strength [H 9]. The original study is unverified.
  - Rodriguez: 15–20°, with no reason given; an outlier against all other sources [H 3].
  - Source [H 9] prints "70°" and "90°"; 7° and 9° are clearly meant.
- **Convention, not strength:** The slope hardly changes strength [H 9][H 10]. Schwarz calls it a matter of style and prefers 1:4 [H 7]. 1:6 works for most projects in both kinds of wood [H 11]. The usual reasoning (softwood crushes, steep tails break in hardwood) appeared only in forums (unverified).
- **For the course:** 1:6 as in the textbook. Tell the apprentice plainly that the hardwood/softwood rule is a convention [H 7][H 9][H 10].

### H4. Marking gauge and baseline

- **Two variants:**
  - **Default: exactly the thickness (SL = D).** This is what the textbook (unverified, title pending) and Cosman [H 13] do. The course follows this variant and has the apprentice set the marking gauge directly off the mating board. SL = D remains the default.
  - **Workshop practice, differs from the textbook (book: SL = D, remains the default).** Many craft sources set the marking gauge slightly larger than the thickness of the mating board, so pins and tails stand proud and can be planed flush later. Myers gives 1/64″ (≈ 0.4 mm) over the stock thickness [H 5], Wittmer about 1/32″ (≈ 0.8 mm) [H 11]; these two passages were additionally re-checked against the text on October 14, 2026. Similar: Frid (≈ 0.4 mm) [H 1], the Klausz method (≈ 0.8 mm) [H 16], and Van Dyke ("a little more") [H 2].
- **All around:** The baseline runs across both faces and both edges. Wittmer makes a single firm pass, because double lines cause errors later [H 11]. Kenney goes over the same track twice, light first, then firm [H 17].
- **Knife or pencil:** For the baseline and the transfer, prefer a knife or the gauge, then darken the scribe line with a fine pencil [H 6][H 11][H 14]. Frid uses a pencil or an awl for the first half [H 1], Van Dyke a fine mechanical pencil [H 2].
- **Reference faces:** A cabinetmaker's triangle on all parts [H 19], plus labels for board, side, and matching corners [H 5]. Boards flat, equal in thickness, end grain square [H 6][H 11].

### H5. Sawing

- **Saw:** Japanese saws cut on the pull stroke and can be thinner; European dovetail saws cut on the push stroke. The straight handle of a Japanese saw is less ergonomic [H 23].
- **Clamping:** Low and tight in the vise, high enough to reach the baseline without re-clamping [H 11][H 21]. Never hold the workpiece by hand [H 23].
- **Stance:** Saw, forearm, and shoulder in one line. Light grip, three fingers on the handle, index finger extended [H 20][H 21].
- **Starting the cut:** Start on the waste side with no downward pressure [H 21]. Thumb as a guide, away from the teeth, short light strokes [H 20][H 23]. Beginner's trick: first a shallow kerf in the end grain, then down the faces; the saw follows the kerf [H 14].
- **Beside the line:** Saw in the waste and leave the line. This is the key to tight joints [H 13][H 20]. Pin cuts are vertical [H 16].
- **Baseline:** Slow down before the line; never cut past it on the show face [H 11][H 20].
- **Practice sequence:** straight line on scrap, stop at a line, leave the line [H 20].

### H6. Chopping

- **Stop at the baseline:** Scribe the line deep and widen it into a shallow notch; the flat of the chisel sits in it [H 17]. Alternatively, clamp a scrap as a guide [H 5].
- **Placement:** Klausz and Huey start just shy of the line, because the bevel pushes the chisel toward it [H 12][H 15][H 16].
- **Half from each side:** To about half the thickness, then flip the board [H 2][H 12][H 15][H 18]. Gochnour and Cosman start on the inside and finish on the outside, so any blowout stays inside [H 13][H 18].
- **Small bites:** Big bites drive the chisel past the baseline [H 11][H 22].
- **Clearing with a fretsaw or coping saw:** Stay 0.8–1.6 mm (1/32–1/16″) above the baseline, then pare down [H 17][H 18]. Which saw is better is disputed [H 11][H 22].
- **Undercut:** A few degrees for easier assembly [H 11][H 12][H 15]; Cosman rejects it [H 13]. Do not undercut the half-pin areas of the tail board [H 5]. For beginners: little or none.
- **Sharp chisel:** A dull chisel crushes end grain instead of cutting it [H 22].

### H7. Transferring, fitting, gluing up

- **Transferring:** Stand the pin board on the tail board lying flat, align it flush, trace the outline [H 1][H 15][H 16]. A shallow rabbet or a clamped strip at the baseline keeps the board seated [H 2][H 13]. On the tail board, the waste lies between the pin outlines [H 15].
- **Test fit:** Disputed. With pencil marks to show pressure points [H 11] or only halfway [H 15]; Cosman warns against pulling a joint apart [H 13]. Pare in small steps [H 6].
- **Gluing up:** Drive the joint home with a caul [H 11][H 13], check the diagonals [H 13][H 16], plane flush from the corners toward the middle once dry [H 12].

### H8. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Typical cause | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Saw drifts | Tight grip, pressure, bent wrist, line not visible [H 20][H 21] | Light grip, no pressure, start a kerf, practice cuts [H 14][H 20][H 21] |
| Sawn past the baseline | Did not slow down [H 20] | Slow down before the line, work down with toe and heel [H 11][H 20] |
| Baseline creeps back | Bites too big, no notch [H 11][H 17][H 22] | Notch, clear waste first, last 0.8 mm right at the line [H 11][H 17][H 18] |
| Blowout when chopping | Chopped through from one side, dull chisel [H 12][H 22] | Half from each side, sharpen [H 12][H 18][H 22] |
| Wavy baseline | Chisel width does not fit, double gauge line [H 11][H 22] | Match spacing to chisels, one line, guide block [H 5][H 11][H 22] |
| Gaps | Line sawn away, inaccurate transfer, too much undercut [H 5][H 11][H 13] | Leave the line, transfer with a knife [H 11][H 20] |
| Too tight, board splits | Tight spots, forced test fit [H 11][H 13] | Separate, read the pressure marks, pare [H 11][H 15] |
| Transferred the wrong way round | Reference faces not marked [H 19] | Cabinetmaker's triangle, X on the waste [H 11][H 15][H 19] |

Learning idea from the sources: date every practice joint, take it apart, and match the error to a row [H 13][H 15].

### H9. Safety

- **Chisel** [H 23]: Clamp the workpiece firmly. Both hands behind the edge. Mallet, not a steel hammer; never strike with the palm. Eyes on the edge. Dull chisels slip more easily.
- **Saw** [H 23]: Never freehand. Thumb only when starting, away from the teeth. Japanese saws can cause deep cuts. Gramercy recommends safety glasses [H 21].
- **Law (Austria):** The verified version of KJBG-VO § 1 (BGBl. II Nr. 436/1998, valid until June 30, 2015) defines supervision and training exemptions [H 24]. The current version is unverified.
- **AR rule (project rule, not a source statement):** The AR headset is used only for laying out and checking. Never saw or chop with the headset on. Reasons: both hands and the eyes belong on saw and edge [H 23]. The passthrough image is distorted at close range [D 28], and depth is judged less accurately [D 27].

## Part D: Didactics

### D1. Core models

- **Four-step method:** Prepare, demonstrate and explain, imitate with correction, practice. Trainer-centered; errors are caught early [D 1]. It goes back to the US program *Training Within Industry – Job Instruction* (1941) [D 2].
- **Cognitive apprenticeship:** Applies craft apprenticeship to learning ways of thinking. Methods: modeling with visible thinking, coaching, scaffolding with fading, articulation, reflection against expert work, exploration [D 3]†.
- **4C/ID:** Whole learning tasks, supportive information (the why, before the task), procedural information (the how, in the moment), and part-task practice [D 4]†.
- **Motor learning:** Three phases: cognitive, associative, autonomous [D 7]†. Deliberate practice with sub-goals and feedback explains differences in performance [D 8]. The associative phase only happens on real wood.

### D2. Cognitive load

- Working memory is tightly limited. Good instruction cuts unnecessary load and leaves room for schema building [D 5].
- More immersion does not mean more learning: in a lab simulation, VR raised presence, lowered learning, and raised measured load [D 17].
- Consequence: keep VR sparse and use it where it adds something (slow motion, perspective, repetition).

### D3. Worked examples

- For novices, worked examples can replace problem solving and lead to faster learning [D 6]†.
- Consequence: the spacing is fully worked out before the apprentice divides a board on their own [D 5][D 6].

### D4. Fading and feedback

- **Guidance hypothesis:** Frequent, immediate augmented feedback improves practice performance. Learners come to rely on it, and without feedback their performance drops in retention or transfer tests [D 10]†. Practice performance and learning must therefore be measured separately.
- **Caveat:** For complex skills, more support early on can help [D 11]†. Visual, auditory, and haptic augmented feedback each have their own design criteria [D 12].
- **Feedback levels:** Feedback is powerful, but not always positive. It should clarify where one stands and what comes next. Praise aimed at the person is the least effective [D 9]†.
- **Consequence:** The AR overlay is ongoing augmented feedback and must be faded: full lines, then endpoints only, then a check after the cut, then nothing [D 10][D 11][D 12].

### D5. AR and VR in the trades

- **Overview:** On average, AR shows a medium effect on learning gains (64 studies) [D 15]; a second meta-analysis exists [D 16]. For VR, games worked better than simulations; the figures are known only from a secondary source [D 14]†. Many VR studies measure usability instead of learning outcome [D 13].
- **Assembly:** AR registered to the workpiece cut errors sharply, especially follow-on errors [D 18]. This supports AR for layout, because layout errors cause follow-on errors. AR training led to fewer errors but longer times [D 19].
- **Welding:** A VR simulator transferred better than video, with a small sample [D 20]. WeldAR also improved performance in an unassisted test [D 22] and is the methodological model. The literature names transfer to real work as the main gap [D 21].
- **Spray painting:** Expert and novice scores were close in real and virtual painting, without statistics [D 23].
- **Wood:** AR improved spatial visualization of mortise-and-tenon joints [D 24]. In vocational school, AR at a milling machine matched teacher-led instruction [D 25]. No study measuring the quality of learners' dovetail joints was found.
- **Headset limits:** Quest 3 usually stays below 1 cm relative pose error, but exceeded 10 cm absolute in hard cases [D 26]. Passthrough is distorted at close range (trade press, no measurement) [D 28]. Everyday tasks in passthrough were slower and more error-prone [D 27]. No millimeter-level measurement of overlay registration on Quest 3 was found.
- **Cybersickness and novelty:** [D 29] reviews causes and measurement; the standard instrument is the SSQ [D 30]. Presence and motivation are not proof of learning [D 17].

### D6. Self-efficacy

- General scale: 10 items, four-point [D 34].
- For a concrete skill, a task-specific scale fits better ("I can lay out a dovetail so that …", 0–100), built following Bandura's guide [D 35].

### D7. Measurement

| Construct | Instrument | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Usability | SUS [D 31] | 10 items, score 0–100 †; norms in [D 32]† |
| Workload | NASA-TLX [D 33] | Six subscales †; to compare conditions |
| Cybersickness | SSQ [D 30] | Before and after every headset session, stop criterion set in advance |
| Self-efficacy | General [D 34], task-specific [D 35] | Pre and post |
| Knowledge | Own test | Separate from the learning quiz |
| Workpiece quality | Own protocol | Joint gap, flank angle, line deviation, time, blind expert rating. No standard in the literature [D 24][D 25] |

Study design rules: measure learning without the overlay, on a real board, and after several days [D 10]. Control condition: four-step instruction [D 1]. Do not substitute usability for learning outcome [D 13]. Several sessions against the novelty effect [D 17]. Details in the [study design](studiendesign.en.md).

### D8. Ethics and law

- Art. 8 GDPR and § 4(4) DSG (age 14) apply to online services offered directly to children, not to a research study [D 36][D 37]. § 7 DSG governs processing for research purposes [D 37].
- Conservative standard: separate information sheets for parents and minors [D 38]. Schools require their own approval and written consent [D 39].
